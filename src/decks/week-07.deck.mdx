# Storage is two problems

## wearing one name

SLOP6246 · Week 7 · duration

```notes
The whole lecture is one distinction: power versus energy. Everything else is
consequence. Ask at the start who can state the difference in units without
thinking about it — usually fewer hands than expected, and that is the point.
```

---

## "How much storage do we need?"

Two questions in a trench coat.

- **How fast** can it deliver? — power
- **How long** can it keep delivering? — energy

A specification that answers one and assumes the other is how a system arrives
at an event under-built.

```notes
Stress that both questions are legitimate and usually asked by different people
for different reasons — a network engineer worries about the first, a planner
about the second. The failure is not asking the wrong question, it is answering
one and reporting it as though it settled both.
```

---

## The units do the arguing

| Quantity   | Measured in | Answers                       |
| ---------- | ----------- | ----------------------------- |
| **Power**  | MW, GW      | Can I meet the shortfall now? |
| **Energy** | MWh, GWh    | Can I keep meeting it?        |

Duration is the ratio: energy ÷ power.

```notes
If students take one slide away, this is it. Watch for the common slip of
writing MW when MWh is meant — in this course that slip is not a typo, it is a
design error that changes the answer.
```

---

## A specification, deliberately absurd

**1 GW / 1 GWh** — illustrative, not a product

Nominal power of 1 GW. Stored energy of 1 GWh.

Duration: **one hour.**

```notes
Say plainly that this is an illustrative configuration invented for the
lecture, not a real battery and not a claim about any product's performance.
It is chosen because the arithmetic is trivial and the conclusion is stark.
```

---

## What one hour of duration buys

At full output it is empty in an hour.

That is genuinely useful — for a frequency event, an evening peak, a
generator tripping offline.

It is the right machine for the wrong problem.

```notes
Be careful to be fair to short-duration storage here. It is not a bad asset;
it is an asset specified against a different failure mode, and it is correct
for the problem it was specified against. Avoid claiming how much storage is
procured that way — this course has no source for that, and does not need one
for the argument to land.
```

## The mismatch

- Event span: **61 hours** *(synthetic trace)*
- Storage duration: **one hour** *(illustrative spec)*

The power rating was never the binding constraint.

```notes
The qualitative point stands on its own here, and the arithmetic comes in the
lecture rather than on the slide: the dispatch scenario does declare a
synthetic demand of 40% of capacity, so the deficit and the energy shortfall
are both computable. Keep that on the board in the lecture, not here — this
slide is making the units argument, and a number on it invites the class to
check the sum instead of the reasoning.
```

---

## Four levers

1. **Power** — how fast it can deliver
2. **Energy** — how much it holds
3. **State of charge** — how full it was when the event started
4. **Round-trip efficiency** — what you lose storing and retrieving

```notes
Lever 3 is the one students consistently forget, and it is the one an operator
actually controls in the days before a forecast event. Connect forward to week
10: pre-charging is a long-lead-time action, so it depends on a forecast
arriving early enough to authorise it.
```

---

## Which lever moves the outcome?

Against a multi-day deficit:

- raising **power** — no, and not approximately
- raising **energy** — yes, until it becomes unaffordable
- starting **fuller** — yes, and it is free if you saw it coming
- better **efficiency** — helps at the margin

```notes
This is the slide to spend time on. Let students argue. The honest answer is
that energy and state of charge dominate, and that the cheapest of the four is
the one that depends entirely on forecasting and institutional willingness to
act early. On power, be exact rather than hedging: swept across this scenario's
window the energy left unserved is the same at 40%, at 60% and at 120% of
capacity. Power redistributes when the shortfall lands, not how large it is.
The lecture works that sweep through.
```

---

## Why the instinct misleads

A store sized for a daily peak is solving the problem a daily cycle poses.

The daily peak is a **power** problem.

A drought is a **duration** problem.

The same word covers both, and the procurement does not transfer.

```notes
This is the week's contribution to the thesis: the event does not simply make
storage more necessary, it changes which specification number matters. A fleet
sized correctly for one problem can be irrelevant to the other.
```

## What this means for your dispatch

In [Assessment 3](/assessments/a3-the-72-hour-dispatch/) you will spend a
finite store across a multi-day event.

Every hour you discharge early is an hour unavailable at hour 60.

```notes
Point out that the dispatch assessment is where this becomes visceral. The
temptation is to hold storage back; the cost of holding it back is load shed
earlier. There is no free ordering, and the log has to own the choice.
```

# Power is not energy

A system sized in megawatts has not answered a question asked in hours.

```notes
Close on the unit distinction, then hand over to the studio exercise where they
manipulate all four levers against a fixed deficit. Remind them the deficit is
synthetic and fixed deliberately so the levers are the only variable.
```
