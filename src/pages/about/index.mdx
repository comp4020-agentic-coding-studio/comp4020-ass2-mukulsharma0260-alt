# About this course

Most renewable-energy teaching is organised by technology: a block on wind, a
block on solar, a block on storage, a block on grids. That structure is easy to
timetable and it quietly teaches the wrong lesson — that reliability is the sum
of a set of components rather than a property of a system meeting a specific
condition.

This course is organised the other way round. One failure condition, held fixed
for twelve weeks, and technologies introduced only where they change how that
condition is defined, detected, predicted, survived, allocated or bounded.

## The condition

A period when wind and solar output are simultaneously low, for long enough
that normal planning assumptions stop holding. The course fixes an operative
definition of it at [the event](/the-event/) — a threshold, a span, a recovery
rule, a scope and a season — so that twelve weeks of argument are about systems
rather than vocabulary.

That definition is deliberately contestable, and
[the first assessment](/assessments/a1-define-the-drought/) asks you to move it
and defend the move.

## How the semester is shaped

Three acts. What the event is, what survives it, and who decides — plus who
explains it afterwards. Each of the twelve weeks owns exactly one property of
the event and no two weeks own the same one, which is the structural rule that
stops the course drifting into a technology survey.

Each week states whether it **advances** the central claim, **complicates** it
or **challenges** it. The challenges are not decoration: week 6 attacks the
most widely trusted mitigation in the field, and week 8 points out that the
last line of defence is bought rather than engineered.

## What the course runs on

One synthetic teaching trace: fourteen days at hourly resolution, generated by
a seeded function that lives in this repository. Every figure, studio exercise
and worked example reads from it, so no two pages can disagree about the
reference event.

It is not measured data. There is no reanalysis or historical record behind it,
and it does not describe any real grid. It exists so that the whole cohort
argues about the same fortnight. Anything derived from it is labelled
**Synthetic trace** wherever it appears, and the course's own chosen boundaries
are labelled **Course-defined**, because those are different kinds of claim.

## What this course does not do

- It does not derive machine equations. No power-systems background is assumed.
- It does not rank technologies. A technology is interesting here only in
  relation to the event.
- It does not present a correct answer. The final studio is a critique of
  designs that are all defensible and all limited.
- It does not use figures from real electricity systems. Where a real empirical
  claim would be needed and no source is available, the course writes
  qualitatively instead of inventing precision.

## What you leave with

A system you specified, operated through an event, and can defend — together
with a precise statement of the event that defeats it. The second half of that
sentence is the part the course actually grades hardest.
