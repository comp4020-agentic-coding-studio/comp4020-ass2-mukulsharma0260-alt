# {frontmatter.title}

Every design in this course is asked for two statements: the event it survives,
and the event that defeats it. This page is where the second statement lives.

It is not a wall of shame, and it is not encouragement. It is a register. A
design whose limits are written down can be checked, compared and improved by
someone who was not in the room. A design that claims to survive everything has
either not been tested or is not telling you about the case it fails.

## Why the course keeps one

The thesis is that a defensible system names its own failure boundary. If that
is true, the boundary is a deliverable — and deliverables get recorded
somewhere durable rather than mentioned once in a studio.

Three things follow from keeping the record in public:

1. **A boundary becomes falsifiable.** "This system survives the reference
   event but not one arriving with storage already depleted" is a claim someone
   can test. "This system is robust" is not.
2. **Comparison becomes possible.** Two portfolios that both survive the
   reference event are not equivalent if one fails at 70 hours and the other at
   140.
3. **The incentive changes.** When the failure boundary is the marked output,
   there is nothing to gain from hiding it.

## What gets recorded

For each entry: the design, the event it survived, the event that defeated it,
and where the boundary was located — in generation, in storage duration, in
transmission, in reserve availability, in forecast lead time, or in the
allocation rules.

The last of those matters more than it looks. [Week 11](/sessions/week-11/)
argues that the cause of a failure is partly a choice about where the system's
edge was drawn, so an entry that does not say where the boundary sits has not
finished describing the failure.

## How entries arrive

From the studios, and from
[Design for the Worst Week](/assessments/a4-design-for-the-worst-week/), where
stating the defeating event is the primary requirement rather than a caveat.

The wall opens empty at the start of semester. Filling it is the work.

## What this page is not

It does not record marks. It does not name students who ran out of storage in
hour 50 — that is what the studio is for, and it happens to most designs at
least once. It records designs and their boundaries, which are technical
objects rather than performances.
